Classical physics had two kinds of thing: particles, which sit in one place and carry momentum, and waves, which spread out and interfere. Experiments between 1900 and 1927 showed that light and matter are each both.

## Light comes in quanta

In the photoelectric effect, light shone on a metal knocks electrons out of it. Brighter light frees more electrons but not faster ones; only the light's frequency sets their energy. Einstein explained this in 1905 by treating light as a stream of photons, each carrying an energy proportional to its frequency $\nu$:

$$
E = h\nu
$$

An electron absorbs one photon and leaves with kinetic energy $E_k = h\nu - \phi$, where $\phi$ is the work function of the metal. Below the threshold frequency $\nu_0 = \phi / h$ no electrons come out at all, however bright the light.

## Matter has a wavelength

In 1924 de Broglie proposed the reverse: anything with momentum $p$ has a wavelength

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p}
$$

For everyday objects this wavelength is far too small to notice. An electron accelerated through 100 V, though, has $\lambda \approx 0.12$ nm, close to the spacing of atoms in a crystal, and in 1927 Davisson and Germer saw electrons diffract off a nickel crystal just as X-rays do.

## Both at once

The double-slit experiment shows both sides in one picture. Particles sent through one at a time land as single dots on the screen, yet the dots build up an interference pattern, as though each one passed through both slits.
