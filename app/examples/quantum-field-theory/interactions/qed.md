Quantum electrodynamics, QED for short, is the field theory of electrons, positrons and photons: everything about light and electricity, except gravity and the nuclear forces. Its rules are the ones on [[id:1m8taa8bjcuyw]]: one kind of vertex, where an electron emits or absorbs a photon, and a strength set by the fine-structure constant,

$$
\alpha^{-1} = 137.035\,999\,177\,(21),
$$

the 2022 value recommended by CODATA. The digits in brackets are the uncertainty in the last two.

## The electron as a magnet

An electron spins and carries charge, so it is a tiny magnet. How strong a magnet, compared with its spin, is measured by a number called $g$. Dirac's 1928 equation for the electron predicted exactly $g = 2$.

QED says the electron is never alone: it keeps emitting and reabsorbing virtual photons, and those change its magnetism slightly. In 1948 Julian Schwinger worked out the first correction, from the simplest loop diagram:

$$
\frac{g}{2} = 1 + \frac{\alpha}{2\pi} + \dots \approx 1.00116
$$

The calculation has since been carried to the fifth power of $\alpha$, which alone takes 12,672 Feynman diagrams. The best measurement, from a single electron held in a trap for months, agrees:

| | $g/2$ |
| --- | --- |
| Dirac, 1928 | $1$ |
| Schwinger's first correction, 1948 | $1.001\,16$ |
| Measured, 2023 | $1.001\,159\,652\,180\,59\,(13)$ |

The 2023 measurement is good to 0.13 parts per trillion. The theory matches it as closely as $\alpha$ itself is known, and that is now the limit: the two best measurements of $\alpha$, made with caesium and rubidium atoms, disagree with each other by more than their stated uncertainties.

## The Lamb shift

QED's first triumph came earlier. Dirac's theory says two energy levels of the hydrogen atom, called 2S and 2P, have exactly the same energy. In 1947 Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford found them apart, by about 1,000 MHz in frequency; the split is now known to be about 1,058 MHz. The split comes from the electron's cloud of virtual photons and the vacuum's virtual electron–positron pairs, and calculating it was one of the problems that forced physicists to deal with the infinities in their loops: see [[id:0sdfzcgnhed5x]].

Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for QED.
