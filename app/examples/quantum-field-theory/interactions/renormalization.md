The first term of the series on [[id:1m8taa8bjcuyw]] is finite. The next terms have loops, and a loop means an integral over every momentum that could run around it, up to infinitely large ones. In the 1930s those integrals came out infinite, and for over a decade it looked as if quantum field theory gave nonsense beyond its first approximation.

## What the infinities were hiding

The way out came from asking what is actually measured. An electron is never seen bare: it is always surrounded by its own cloud of virtual photons and virtual electron–positron pairs. The charge and mass we measure belong to the electron *with* its cloud. The "bare" charge and mass in the equations are never seen at all.

**Renormalization** rewrites every prediction in terms of the measured charge and mass instead of the bare ones. When that is done, the infinities from the loops cancel against the infinite difference between bare and measured values, and every prediction that can be compared with experiment comes out finite. The precise agreement on [[id:2mm217t2bow3t]] is calculated this way.

## Forces that change with energy

Renormalization leaves a real, measurable effect behind. The cloud of virtual pairs around an electron partly screens its charge, like a crowd around a celebrity: from far away you see less of them. Probe the electron at higher energy, which means closer in, and you see more of its charge. So the strength of electromagnetism grows with energy:

| Energy | $1/\alpha$ |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday energies | about 137 |
| The mass of the Z boson, about 91 GeV | about 128 |

This is called a **running coupling**. The strong force, which holds quarks together, runs the other way: it gets weaker at high energy, because its gluons pile up around a quark in a way that spreads the charge out rather than screening it. This **asymptotic freedom** is why quarks inside a proton, hit hard enough, behave almost as free particles. The flip side is that at low energies the force grows so strong that a quark is never seen on its own. David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics for discovering it.

## Theories as approximations

The modern view, due largely to Kenneth Wilson, is gentler on the old infinities. A field theory is understood as a description valid up to some energy, beyond which something new may take over. Renormalization is then the bookkeeping of how a theory's constants change as the energy you look at changes, and the infinities were a sign of pretending the theory held at every energy at once.
